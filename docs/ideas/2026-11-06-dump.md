## Codes and Containers
QR code contains a URL.
The URL contains a secret.
The id of the code is not the id of the object.
Perhaps using a URL fragment (hash sign) because I believe that would keep the secret out of browser history.
The flow probably implies 1. opening the link that's stored in the QR code (which contains a secret) and 2. the opened page will redirect you to the URL of the currently linked resource (e.g. a seed tray with yellow pear shaped tomatoes, a flower pot with arugula seeded in it, a line of carrots in my vegetable plot...)

The QR code and resource are decoupled to enable printing a large batch of QR codes at once. (Single run to the print shop)
High priority is generating codes on the screen to be able to test the flow end to end.
Lower priority is outputting an A4 tailored to the specific pre-cut sticker paper I'll buy to test with.
Lowest priority is everything related to supporting different page layouts, pre-cut templates, paper formats, label printers, etc.
Monetization opportunity once multiple gardeners start using it: buy sets of QR codes from PlantBook, or buy plant labels with QR codes on it from PlantBook.

QR code tradeoffs:
- physical size of the code
- ease of scanning for different types of phones (also low end)
- ability to error correct
- storing more data in the code means stronger secret, larger namespace
  - if we choose stateless (UUID style), take into account chance of collision

seed trays, pots, etc. are different instances of the same concept
QR codes could be stuck on trays, pots, containers, egg cartons, square meter gardens, sections of a vegetable garden (e.g. by means of a code on a plant marker)...
We need a good name for this concept / entity.
E.g. a rubarb plant could also receive a code next to it. At some point that plant might be lifted out of the ground and divided. Each of the, say, 4 parts would get a code and the page of each child plant would be able to display or link to the history of the mother plant.

So there are codes that at a given point in time may or may not be attached to something.
What they're attached to could be relatively permanent (e.g. a sticker on a 4cm pot or a planting tray)
or it could change over time (e.g. a code on a planting label that one season sticks in a pot of marigolds and next season in front of a row of carrots)

The thing a code is currently attached to can be relatively freeform I suppose.
It's the unit that makes sense for the gardener.
It could contain one or more plant histories (e.g. a planter could contain lettuce AND radishes, a seeding tray could contain tomatoes AND peppers).
When the tomatoes get transplanted, the history of the peppers is less important to the transplanted tomatoes, so we wouldn't display that on the page of a transplanted tomato.
When we click through to the history of the seed tray, there we would see that these tomatoes were in the same seed tray from date X to date Y.
Some updates on the seed tray may apply to the entire tray and other to one type of plant only.

## Accounts and Plant Sharing
Guiding principle: ease of use

PlantBook starts and ends in the physical world. Real human interactions and real plants.

The conversation doesn't start as: I created an app for plants bla bla, here you can create an account, this is how you can use it...

The conversation starts as: I've been growing these plants, it's more than I can fit in my balcony or garden, would you like to adopt some? :D The plant happens to have a QR code on it, the friend scans it and sees the history of the plant, the exact variety, pictures, etc. and discovers that they can post updates too.

The conversation could also start as: I have these seeds and plant stock available. Would you like me to try and propagate a rosemary for you? That's very kind, and I'd like to try some pepper plants on my kitchen window this year. I saw they grew well for you last season.

On an individual level, PlantBook is a convenient and fun way to keep track of the plants that you're growing, and to improve as a gardener by journaling and learning from your observations.

On an inter-personal level, PlantBook encourages gifting, meeting up with the people around you, and getting more hands and plants in the ground.

Success looks like:
- People bond over plants that they both took care of at different phases.
- Someone who received a plant this season, picks up growing plants next season, and ends up in a position to share plants (or produce) with their friends. They feel supported by a network of gardeners built on generosity, care and kindness.
- Gardeners start exchanging seeds and plant stock, increasing the diversity in everyone's collection at a lower cost and while reducing wasted plant material.
- Gardeners start seed saving circles, community gardens, cooking groups, tool sharing...

The desired interaction requires ease-of-use and the least amount of friction possible. The technology stays out of the way so the human interaction can unfold and flourish. PlantBook needs to stay out of the way until people invite it into their life.

Therefore the QR code implies edit rights by default. (If later a use case arises, the "owner" could make it read-only for people that are not logged in). The (secret in the) QR code needs to stay offline to prevent spamming from strangers. The plant pages itself are publicly accessible (read-only). Later, as the need arises, updates, plant pages or profiles could receive more granular visibility settings (e.g. public, friends only, private...)

I want to support user accounts via passwordless authentication (magic link or code via email). Plants can be linked to user profiles, such that users can view an overview of their plants without scanning QR codes, keep access to their plants even if a QR code gets lost or damaged, and continue to see updates after they give plants away.
